When you have a small sample (say n=10) and you observe a large effect (r = -0.886), you cannot trust that number as the true population effect. Small samples produce noisy estimates — you might have happened to get 10 test methods where your pipeline performed exceptionally well, and the true average effect across all possible test methods is actually more modest.

This is called small-sample inflation bias. It is a well-documented phenomenon: the smaller your sample, the more your observed effect size tends to overshoot the true one.
